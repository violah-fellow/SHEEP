General Definition
Alternative proteins are plant-based, fermentation-derived, or cultivated alternatives to protein-rich animal-derived foods, including both novel ingredients (e.g. single-cell proteins) and direct analogues of meat, seafood, dairy, and egg products. Substitution may be at the ingredient level (e.g. a plant protein replacing whey) or the product level (e.g. plant-based yoghurt replacing dairy yoghurt), and protein need not be explicitly stated as a goal. Publications are in scope if they fall into either or both of the following two categories:
- Studies on the societal, policy, regulatory, consumer acceptance, environmental, life-cycle, or techno-economic aspects of alternative proteins.
- Research that advances the development, characterisation, understanding, perception, or acceptance of alternative proteins or the processes used to produce them.

Category Definitions and Labels
Plant-based (including fermented plant ingredients): Research on plant or macroalgal species either as sources of protein or as functional ingredients for human food, including protein functionality, processing effects, crop or strain optimisation, and plant-based meat, dairy, egg, or seafood analogues. Note: traditional fermentation used only as a processing method to modify a plant protein (fermented plant material) is classified as plant-based, not fermentation.
Fermentation (biomass and precision): Research where microbial, fungal, single-cell, mycoprotein biomass is the protein source (biomass fermentation) or where microorganisms recombinantly produce specific proteins such as whey protein, heme, enzymes (precision fermentation), including the optimisation of strains, fermentation processes, and feedstocks for either route. Note: traditional fermentation used only as a processing method to modify a plant protein (fermented plant material) is classified as plant-based, not fermentation.
Fermentation (biomass and precision): Research on the production of protein for human food via: biomass fermentation, in which microbial, fungal, or microalgae biomass is itself the protein source (single-cell protein, mycoprotein); or precision fermentation, in which microorganisms are used to recombinantly produce specific proteins (whey protein, heme, enzymes).
Cultivated meat: Research on the cultivation of animal cells, tissues, or fat in vitro for human food, including cell line development, cell culture media, scaffolding, and bioprocessing methods for cultivated meat, seafood, or dairy.
Cross-cutting: Only for publications that address alternative proteins broadly without being specific to one pillar — typically societal, policy, regulatory, consumer acceptance, environmental, or food systems research. Do not combine with other pillar labels.
Where a publication explicitly covers multiple protein sources or production methods, assign all relevant pillar labels (plant-based, fermentation, cultivated).

Scope Boundaries
- Technological research must concern ingredients, products, or processes with a stated or intended application in alternative protein development. This may include non-protein components or functional ingredients (e.g. lipids, fibres, hydrocolloids, enzymes) intended for alternative protein products.
- Socioeconomic research (policy, regulation, consumer behaviour, market analysis, environmental impact, life-cycle assessment, techno-economic analysis) is in scope if alternative proteins are explicitly named and meaningfully discussed, even if not the primary focus.
- Hybrid products combining alternative and animal proteins are in scope when the research is relevant to alternative protein development, and not solely to improve conventional animal protein products.
- Comparisons of alternative proteins with animal proteins are in scope when alternative protein ingredients or products are explicitly named and studied (e.g. plant-based meat in dietary or health outcome publications). 
- Biochemical properties (flavour, aroma, nutrition, allergenicity) are in scope only if the implied use case is substituting animal products.
- Enabling technologies (e.g. scaffolding materials and biomaterials for cultivated meat, cell culture components, extraction or processing methods, encapsulation systems) are in scope if alternative protein production is a primary or significant stated application, not merely a speculative or incidental mention.
- Agronomic research (e.g. crop yield, intercropping, organic farming practices, variety selection) is in scope when it concerns high-protein crops used as alternative protein feedstocks (e.g. pea, faba bean, soy, lupin, lentil, chickpea).
- General nutritional products for healthy populations (infant formula, products targeting children or adolescents) are in scope. 

Out of Scope Topics
The following do not qualify as alternative proteins and should not contribute to an in-scope classification on their own. If a publication covers in-scope alternative protein content as well as any of these topics, it is still classified as in scope and labelled accordingly.
- Insect-based proteins
- Non-human food applications (animal feed, aquafeed, fish food, seafood feed, pet food, biofuels, pharmaceuticals)
- Foods that do not substitute animal products (e.g. bread, pasta, snacks)
- Broad dietary pattern studies (e.g. plant-based, vegan, vegetarian, Mediterranean diets) where specific alternative protein products or ingredients are not named or studied
- General agronomic research on staple crops where protein is not the primary value (e.g. wheat, rice, maize)
- Medical nutrition or products targeting diagnosed medical conditions (nutraceuticals, bioactive peptides, therapeutic formulas)
- Meat analogues or hybrid products where the primary protein source is animal-derived
